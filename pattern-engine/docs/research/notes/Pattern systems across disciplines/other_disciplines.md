# Pattern systems in non-software disciplines: procedure templates, selection, and the "no procedure fits" rule

Scope: aviation, military, medicine, cooking, and general problem-solving methods. For each discipline, the notes give five facets:

| Facet | Question |
|---|---|
| Template | What does one procedure contain? |
| Relations | How do procedures relate: hierarchy, branch, reference? |
| Selection | How does the practitioner find the right procedure? |
| Split | Which part is deterministic, and which part is judgment? |
| No-fit rule | What is the explicit rule when no procedure fits? |

Source note: many PDFs (NASA NTRS scans, the WHO manual, FAA AC 120-71B) did not give readable text to the fetch tool. Where that happened, the notes use secondary sources and say so.

## Aviation: QRH, checklists, memory items, and the unlisted failure

### Takeaway
Aviation has a strict hierarchy. Normal checklists use "do-verify". Non-normal checklists use "read-do", with memory items first when time is critical. The QRH holds the non-normal checklists, and an alert message (EICAS) is the index key. If no checklist fits, two rules apply: the priority order "aviate, navigate, communicate", and the Boeing rule that the captain combines checklists and uses judgment.

### Cited Findings
- **Origin of the research.** Degani and Wiener studied the normal checklist for NASA Ames (1990). They examined its functions, format, design, length and use, and the limits of its users. They wrote that the checklist "has escaped the scrutiny of the human factors profession". The report ends with design guidelines for normal checklists. — [NASA NTRS 19910017830](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19910017830)
- The work started after the NTSB investigation of the Northwest Airlines MD-82 crash. At that investigation, Wiener testified "that he did not know of any human factors research on how a checklist should be designed." — [AIN Online, 2023](https://oldain.ainonline.com/aviation-news/business-aviation/2023-03-01/best-practices-checklist-design-account-human-limitations)
- The journal article is Degani and Wiener, "Cockpit Checklists: Concepts, Design, and Use", *Human Factors* 35(2), 345–359 (1993). The data came from 42 crews on 72 flights, about 140 flight hours. The article also covers airline culture, CRM and production pressure. — [Degani PDF, thehumandiver.com mirror](https://www.thehumandiver.com/resource_redirect/downloads/sites/3839/themes/2022276/downloads/VauqPYTC8jFaUycRTgrf_Degani_-_Human_Factors_The_Journal_of_the_Human_Factor.pdf); [NTRS 19930068509](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930068509)
- In 1994, a second NASA contractor report by Degani and Wiener, "On the Design of Flight-Deck Procedures", extended this work from checklists to procedures in general. The fetch tool could not read the scanned text. — [NTRS 19940029437 PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19940029437/downloads/19940029437.pdf)
- **Method per checklist type (secondary source).** Normal procedures use "do-verify". Abnormal procedures are strictly "read-do". Time-critical emergency procedures start with memory items, and then a "read-do" check follows. The FAA Practical Test Standards support "do-verify" for normal checklists. — [Flying Magazine, "Put down your checklist"](https://www.flyingmag.com/put-down-your-checklist/)
- Most operators use "do-verify" for normal checklists and "challenge-do-verify" for abnormal and emergency checklists. FAA inspector guidance tells POIs not to approve "do-verify" for non-normal or emergency procedures. The excerpt is cut off, so the exception clause is not known. — [PRISM Research Brief, Cockpit Checklist Fundamentals](https://safetymaterials.argus.aero/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/PRISM-Research-Brief-Cockpit-Checklist-Fundementals.pdf)
- "Challenge-do-verify" has three parts. One crewmember gives a challenge before the action. Then the action occurs. Then the crew verifies that the item is complete. — [PRISM Research Brief](https://safetymaterials.argus.aero/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/PRISM-Research-Brief-Cockpit-Checklist-Fundementals.pdf)
- The governing FAA document is AC 120-71B, "Standard Operating Procedures and Pilot Monitoring Duties for Flight Deck Crewmembers", dated 10 January 2017. It is written for Part 121 and 135 operators. The fetch tool did not read its text in this session. — [FAA AC 120-71B](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_120-71B.pdf)
- **QRH structure.** The 737 FCOM says the QRH holds all non-normal checklists and in-flight performance data. — [737NG FCOM](https://www.737ng.co.uk/737NG%20POH.pdf)
- In a printed Boeing non-normal checklist, items above a dashed line are memory items. Items below the line are reference items. A checklist with no EICAS message is an "unannunciated" checklist. This source is a study aid, not an official manual. — [Brainscape, QRH non-normal checklist instructions](https://www.brainscape.com/flashcards/qrh-non-normal-checklist-instructions-8576380/packs/14596906)
- **Design philosophy.** A NASA symposium presentation on the Boeing QRH says that the QRH rests on a "philosophy of use". That philosophy makes assumptions about crew competence and uses models of human performance. The presentation lists decisions and problem-solving for situations with no checklist as a training issue. — [NASA Ames, Boeing QRH presentation](https://humanfactors.arc.nasa.gov/flightcognition/download/EAS_Symposium_Presentations/Boeing_QRH.pdf)
- **The Boeing no-fit rule (secondary source).** Boeing QRH text, as quoted on a forum, says three things. It is not possible to write checklists for every possible situation. In multiple-failure cases, crews can combine elements of several checklists. The captain must assess the situation and use good judgment to choose the safest course. This is a forum quote, so compare it with the current QRH. — [PPRuNe post](https://www.pprune.org/10425977-post2252.html) (HTTP 403 in this session; content from the search snippet)
- **The priority order.** "Pilots must first maintain control of the aircraft, navigate away from immediate terrain or obstruction hazards, and only then communicate." — [Open Exam Prep, FAA sport pilot guide 7.3](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/faa-sport-pilot/ch7-airport-ops-adm-emergencies/emergency-procedures-equipment-failures-imc)
- A CFI reference says that these priorities are continuous processes that never stop, and that the pilot uses judgment to put the steps in order. First, the pilot does the immediate action items. When the aircraft is under control, the pilot does the POH emergency checklist from the start. — [CFI Notebook, Emergency Procedures](https://www.cfinotebook.net/notebook/flight-hazards-and-safety/emergency-procedures)
- A Safe Pilots presenter guide calls "aviate, navigate, communicate" a popular checklist that gives the critical priorities for the PIC. — [SafePilots presenter guide, 2013](https://www.safepilots.org/library/contributed/Presenter_Guide_LOC_Open_Forum_30Aug2013.pdf)
- The FAA Airplane Flying Handbook, chapter 18, covers emergency landings. It says that the manufacturer procedures in the POH/AFM take precedence. — [FAA AFH ch. 18](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/19_afh_ch18.pdf)
- **The limit of "unalerted" events.** A NASA/ISAP 2015 paper compared airline checklists for smoke, fire and fumes events with no alert against an industry template. — [NASA ISAP 2015](https://human-factors.arc.nasa.gov/flightcognition/Publications/ISAP_2015_Unalerted_SFF_CL.pdf)
- ALPA safety committee members said that the Boeing smoke and fire checklists put "isolate the source" before "land as soon as possible". They said that an integrated checklist would be better. Delta and Boeing replied that the checklists are the industry standard. — [Defense Daily](https://defensedaily.com/pilots-claim-new-checklists-could-compound-emergencies/uncategorized)

### Inferences
- **Template:** a trigger (an alert message or a condition statement), memory items above a line, reference items below the line, decision branches ("if ... go to step N"), and an end.
- **Relations:** three levels. Normal checklists are in the FCOM. Non-normal checklists are in the QRH. Memory items are a subset of the non-normal checklists. A checklist can refer to another checklist. The POH/AFM has priority over generic handbook guidance.
- **Selection:** deterministic when there is an alert, because the EICAS message names the checklist. For an unannunciated failure, the crew uses a QRH index by condition. Thus, the hard case is the event with no alert.
- **Split:** the action items are deterministic. The choice between checklists, their order (for example, isolate the source or land now), and the decision to combine them are judgment. The priority order "aviate, navigate, communicate" gives a fixed frame for that judgment.
- **No-fit rule:** keep the aircraft under control first. Then combine parts of the existing checklists. The captain decides the safest course. The phrase "the checklist ends, airmanship begins" is a popular summary of this rule. This session did not find it in a primary FAA or Boeing source.

- This session did not find the ICAO guidance (Doc 8168 PANS-OPS, or ICAO Annex 6 on checklists).

## Military: battle drills, SOPs, MDMP, mission command and commander's intent, OODA

### Takeaway
The military has a range of procedures. Battle drills have no deliberate decision. MDMP is a seven-step planning process for new situations. Commander's intent tells subordinates the purpose and the end state. "Disciplined initiative" is the explicit rule for the case when the orders no longer fit the situation.

### Cited Findings
- **Battle drill, definition.** FM 7-1 defines a battle drill as "a collective action rapidly executed without applying a deliberate decision-making process." FM 25-101 uses the same definition. ARTEP 7-8-DRILL applies it to a platoon or a smaller unit. — [Wikipedia, Battle drill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_drill)
- A drill starts on a cue: an enemy action or an order from the leader. It is a trained response to that cue. It needs a minimum of orders from the leader, and it is the same in all Army units. — [Wikipedia, Battle drill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_drill); [GlobalSecurity, FM 7-8 ch. 4](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/policy/army/fm/7-8/ch4.htm)
- Commentators say that a drill buys time for the leader to make a better, more flexible decision. It gives the unit an action to start immediately, so that the unit keeps the initiative. — [Warfare Mastery, "The Problem with Traditional Battle Drills"](https://warfaremastery.substack.com/p/the-problem-with-traditional-battle); [Quartermaster, QMN067](https://quartermaster.substack.com/p/battle-drills-tactical-excellence)
- **MDMP has seven steps:** receipt of mission, mission analysis, COA development, COA analysis (wargame), COA comparison, COA approval, and orders production. — [Army Engineer, Table 1-1 Steps in the MDMP](https://armyengineer.tpub.com/EN5708/EN%2057080005.htm); [Wikiwand, MDMP](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Military_Decision_Making_Process)
- Commanders can make the MDMP steps shorter when time is short, and still make a satisfactory plan. — [Wikiwand, MDMP](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Military_Decision_Making_Process)
- The inputs to receipt of mission are a higher headquarters plan, a new mission, or an expected mission. The output is the initial guidance from the commander. In COA development, the staff makes several different ways to do the mission. — [The Lightning Press, MDMP](https://www.thelightningpress.com/military-decision-making-process-mdmp/)
- **Mission command (ADP 6-0, 2012, archived).** The six principles: "Build cohesive teams through mutual trust. Create shared understanding. Provide a clear commander's intent. Exercise disciplined initiative. Use mission orders. Accept prudent risk." — [ADP 6-0 (2012), UNT web archive](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130213211329/http:/armypubs.army.mil/doctrine/DR_pubs/dr_a/pdf/adp6_0_new.pdf)
- The 2012 ADP 6-0 says that commanders use the mission command philosophy and disciplined initiative to respond to unanticipated problems. Every Soldier must act resourcefully within the commander's intent. — [ADP 6-0 (2012), UNT web archive](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130213211329/http:/armypubs.army.mil/doctrine/DR_pubs/dr_a/pdf/adp6_0_new.pdf); [Army.mil, Understanding mission command](https://www.army.mil/article/106872/understanding_mission_command)
- **Commander's intent (doctrine as paraphrased on study cards).** "The commander's intent is a clear and concise expression of the purpose of the operation and the desired military end state." It helps subordinates get the result without more orders, also when the plan changes. — [Brainscape, ADP/ADRP 6-0](https://www.brainscape.com/flashcards/1738944/packs/3012880)
- **The no-fit rule (doctrine as paraphrased on study cards).** "Disciplined initiative is action in the absence of orders, when existing orders no longer fit the situation, or when unforeseen opportunities or threats arise." — [Brainscape, ADP 6-0](https://www.brainscape.com/flashcards/adp-6-0-adp-6-0-mission-command-2333767/packs/4116240)
- **OODA.** John Boyd, a USAF colonel, made the OODA loop in the early 1970s. Decisions occur "in an iterative cycle" of observe, orient, decide and act. An actor that does the cycle faster or better than the opponent can "get inside the opponent's decision cycle". The loop uses continuous feedback, so it permits late commitment. PDCA needs early commitment. — [Wikipedia, OODA loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OODA_loop)
- A critic (Hankins, 2021) says the loop is "vague enough that its defenders and attackers can each see what they want to see in it". — [Wikipedia, OODA loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OODA_loop)

### Inferences
- **Template:** a battle drill has a cue, a fixed sequence of collective actions and standards, and almost no leader decisions. A mission order has a task, a purpose ("in order to"), an intent (purpose and end state) and constraints.
- **Relations:** a spectrum by time and novelty. Drills (no decision) and SOPs are at one end. Abbreviated MDMP is in the middle. Full MDMP is at the other end. Intent travels down the chain of command, so the intent of a higher commander limits the actions of a lower one.
- **Selection:** a drill starts on a recognized cue. MDMP starts on receipt of a mission. The commander can shorten MDMP when time is short.
- **Split:** the drill is deterministic. MDMP is a fixed process with judgment content: the staff makes and compares several COAs. Commander's intent is the fixed reference for the judgment.
- **No-fit rule:** when the orders no longer fit, act without orders, but within the commander's intent (the purpose and the end state). This is the clearest explicit no-fit rule in the five disciplines. The drill also buys time for a later decision.

### Gaps
- The current ADP 6-0 (2019) exact wording was not fetched. The quotes above come from the 2012 edition and from study cards. Check the text at armypubs.army.mil.
- This session did not read the current FM 5-0 text on MDMP.
- This session did not find primary text on Boyd's "Orient" step and "implicit guidance and control" (in "The Essence of Winning and Losing", 1995, or Osinga 2007).

## Medicine: clinical algorithms, the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, GRADE, and clinical judgment

### Takeaway
Medicine has three kinds of procedures: flowchart algorithms (ACLS), team checklists at pause points (WHO), and graded recommendations (GRADE). GRADE contains the explicit no-fit rule. A strong recommendation applies to "most patients". A weak recommendation tells the clinician that "different choices will be appropriate" for each patient, so the patient values decide.

### Cited Findings
- **ACLS algorithm template (AHA 2025).** The adult cardiac arrest algorithm is a flowchart with 12 numbered boxes. Box 1 is "start CPR, attach monitor/defibrillator". The first decision is "Rhythm shockable?". Yes goes to the VF/pVT branch (shock, then 2 minutes of CPR with IV/IO access, then check the rhythm again). No goes to the asystole/PEA branch (epinephrine as soon as possible). The rhythm can go back to the shockable branch. — [AHA 2025 ACLS Cardiac Arrest Algorithm](https://cpr.heart.org/-/media/CPR-Files/CPR-Guidelines-Files/2025-Algorithms/Algorithm-ACLS-CA-250527.pdf?sc_lang=en); [AHA accessible text version](https://cpr.heart.org/-/media/CPR-Files/CPR-Guidelines-Files/2025-Accessible/Algorithm-ACLS-CA-LngDscrp-250725-Ed.pdf?sc_lang=en)
- The algorithm has fixed timings: epinephrine every 3 to 5 minutes, then amiodarone or lidocaine as the antiarrhythmic. It also says "consider" an advanced airway and capnography. It lists reversible causes, for example tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, toxins, and pulmonary and coronary thrombosis. — [AHA 2025 ACLS Cardiac Arrest Algorithm](https://cpr.heart.org/-/media/CPR-Files/CPR-Guidelines-Files/2025-Algorithms/Algorithm-ACLS-CA-250527.pdf?sc_lang=en)
- **WHO Surgical Safety Checklist.** The 2009 implementation manual has a section "Modifying the Checklist". The checklist has three phases: Sign In, Time Out and Sign Out. — [WHO, Implementation manual 2009](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241598590); [Slideshare, manual contents](https://de.slideshare.net/PaulMarkPilar1/implementation-manual-who-surgical-safety-checklist-2009)
- WHO says: "to ensure successful implementation, it is important to make sure that the Checklist is suitable for your setting." — [WHO, Safe surgery tools and resources](https://www.who.int/teams/integrated-health-services/patient-safety/research/safe-surgery/tool-and-resources)
- The first-edition manual says that the checklist is "not intended to be comprehensive". It permits additions and changes for the local setting. — [WHO manual PDF, Leapfrog mirror](https://www.leapfroggroup.org/sites/default/files/Files/WHO_SafeSurgeryChecklistImplementationManual.pdf) (reported by search; the fetch tool could not read the PDF text)
- If a team deletes some items, the checklist will probably become less effective. This is true especially for the items that make teamwork and communication better. — [Harvard DASH report](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/d74f7fd4-5d80-4736-851e-0bfbb67297c9/content)
- A UK pilot found that specialties want to add items that are relevant to them. — [BMJ Quality & Safety, UK pilot experience](https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/lookup/ijlink/YTozOntzOjQ6InBhdGgiO3M6MTQ6Ii9sb29rdXAvaWpsaW5rIjtzOjU6InF1ZXJ5IjthOjQ6e3M6ODoibGlua1R5cGUiO3M6NDoiRlVMTCI7czoxMToiam91cm5hbENvZGUiO3M6MzoiYm1qIjtzOjU6InJlc2lkIjtzOjE3OiIzNDAvamFuMTNfMi9iNTQzMyI7czo0OiJhdG9tIjtzOjE4OiIvcWhjLzIyLzgvNjM5LmF0b20iO31zOjg6ImZyYWdtZW50IjtzOjA6IiI7fQ==)
- **Gawande, *The Checklist Manifesto* (secondary summaries).** A checklist needs a clear point of use, a "pause point", that is a natural break in the work. There are two types. In DO-CONFIRM, the team does the work from memory and then stops to confirm. In READ-DO, the team does each task as it reads it, like a recipe. — [Graham Mann, book notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/the-checklist-manifesto-atul-gawande); [Tallyfy summary](https://tallyfy.com/checklist-manifesto)
- A good checklist has five to nine items. It focuses on the "killer items": the steps that are most dangerous to skip and that people sometimes forget. The idea comes from the Boeing engineer Daniel Boorman. — [Graham Mann, book notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/the-checklist-manifesto-atul-gawande); [Joel Hooks notes](https://joelhooks.com/the-checklist-manifesto)
- After about 60 to 90 seconds at one pause point, the checklist often becomes a distraction. Some summaries say 1 to 2 minutes. — [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/the-checklist-manifesto-pdf-atul-gawande); [Graham Mann, book notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/the-checklist-manifesto-atul-gawande)
- **GRADE strength of recommendation.** Strength is a judgment about whether the desirable effects are more than the undesirable effects. Uncertainty about that balance, "because of low quality evidence or because the desirable and undesirable effects are closely balanced", gives a weak recommendation. — [Guyatt et al., BMJ 2008, GRADE series](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376019)
- Evidence quality and strength are two different judgments. In the warfarin example, the evidence quality is high, but "patients with varying values and preferences will make different choices". Thus, the panel can make a weak recommendation. — [Guyatt et al., BMJ 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376019)
- Implications for clinicians. Strong: "most patients should receive the recommended course of action". Weak: clinicians "should recognise that different choices will be appropriate for each patient", and "clinicians must ensure that patients' care is in keeping with their values and preferences". For a weak recommendation, the discussion of the options with patients and families is the key step. — [Guyatt et al., BMJ 2008, "Going from evidence to recommendations"](https://www.doi.org/10.1136/BMJ.39493.646875.AE); [Children's Oncology Group, GRADE weak recommendations](https://www.childrensoncologygroup.org/downloads/GRADE_COG_weak_recs_26Jul2018.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Template:** (1) An algorithm is a flowchart: numbered boxes, yes/no decision nodes, loops on a timer, and a side list of causes to check. (2) A WHO checklist is a list of items per pause point, read aloud by the team. (3) A guideline recommendation has a statement, a direction (for or against), a strength (strong or weak) and an evidence quality.
- **Relations:** the algorithm branches inside itself, and the branches can loop back. Guidelines are a source for algorithms and checklists. The WHO checklist is a fixed core that local sites can extend. Additions are permitted. Deletions decrease its effect.
- **Selection:** the patient state is the key. For example, "cardiac arrest" selects the ACLS algorithm, and the rhythm selects the branch. The WHO checklist is selected by the phase of the operation (before anesthesia, before incision, before the patient leaves the room).
- **Split:** the action sequence and the drug timings are deterministic. "Consider" items, the search for reversible causes, and all weak recommendations are judgment.
- **No-fit rule:** GRADE gives the rule. A strong recommendation is the default for "most patients". A weak recommendation tells the clinician to choose with the patient. The WHO manual says that the checklist is "not intended to be comprehensive", so it leaves other risks to the team.

### Gaps
- This session did not find the exact AHA sentence that says guidelines do not replace clinical judgment. A search for "clinical judgment" in the AHA 2025 guidelines did not find it.
- This session did not find a primary source on "deviation from guideline" documentation (for example, a medico-legal rule that a clinician records the reason for a deviation).
- The exact WHO manual text (the role of the Checklist coordinator, the read-aloud rule, the exact Sign In / Time Out / Sign Out timing) was not readable.
- The Gawande facts come from summaries, not from the book.

## Cooking: recipes, mise en place, mother sauces, ratios, and chef judgment

### Takeaway
Cooking has a hierarchy of patterns. Mother sauces are base patterns. A small sauce is a mother sauce plus one or two ingredients. Ruhlman's ratios are generative patterns: a change in the proportions changes one dish into a different dish. If no recipe fits, the cook uses a ratio and judgment.

### Cited Findings
- **Mother sauces.** The five mother sauces are béchamel, velouté, espagnole, hollandaise and tomato. — [EHL Insights, Mother sauces](https://hospitalityinsights.ehl.edu/5-mother-sauces); [Escoffier School, five mother sauces](https://escoffier.edu/blog/recipes/how-to-make-the-five-mother-sauces)
- The list changed over time. Carême had allemande in his list. Escoffier replaced it with hollandaise. Escoffier's book called the sauces "Grandes et Petites sauces". One source says that Escoffier put hollandaise in the small sauces. — [Foodie.com, Escoffier and the 5 mother sauces](https://foodie.com/1775974/five-mother-sauces-auguste-escoffier); [Tasting Table](https://www.tastingtable.com/1143868/french-cuisines-5-mother-sauces-explained)
- Derivative sauces are also called daughter, small or secondary sauces. A cook makes them when they add ingredients to a mother sauce, sometimes only one or two ingredients. — [Tasting Table, 3 techniques](https://tastingtable.com/1154290/the-3-techniques-that-turn-french-mother-sauces-into-small-sauces); [Rouxbe, mother sauces](https://rouxbe.com/tips-techniques/333)
- **Ratios.** Ruhlman wants to free home cooks from what he calls the "shackles of the recipe". — [NPR, "Throw Away the Recipe, Learn the Ratio Instead", 2009](https://www.npr.org/2009/07/26/107019060/throw-away-the-recipe-learn-the-ratio-instead)
- Bread is 5 parts flour to 3 parts water, plus yeast and salt. Pie dough ("3-2-1") is 3 parts flour, 2 parts fat and 1 part water. The fat makes pie dough tender, not chewy like bread dough. — [NPR, 2009](https://www.npr.org/2009/07/26/107019060/throw-away-the-recipe-learn-the-ratio-instead); [Scotch & Scones, recipe ratios](https://www.scotchandscones.com/recipe-ratios/)
- The main idea of the book: a change of the ratio changes bread dough into pasta dough. — [Michael Nagrant, review of *Ratio*](https://www.michaelnagrant.com/reviews/cracking-the-code-michael-ruhlman-ratio)

### Inferences
- **Template:** a recipe has a yield, a list of ingredients with quantities (often in the order of use), and a method in numbered steps. A ratio is a shorter template: the proportions of a few base ingredients by weight. The cook adds the flavors.
- **Relations:** a two-level hierarchy, mother sauce to small sauce. A small sauce refers to its mother and adds a delta. Ratios are one level higher. One ratio makes a family of recipes.
- **Selection:** the cook selects the base by its components. For example, a white sauce with milk is a béchamel. Then the cook selects the delta for the dish.
- **Split:** the base ratio and the technique are deterministic. The seasoning, the doneness and the final adjustment ("taste and adjust") are judgment.
- **No-fit rule:** if no recipe fits, find the nearest base (a mother sauce or a ratio), and change one or two elements.

### Gaps
- This session did not find primary text on mise en place as a defined step of the recipe template (for example, in Escoffier's *Le Guide Culinaire* or in a culinary school curriculum).
- This session did not find the exact definition of "ratio" in Ruhlman's book.
- This session did not find an explicit published rule on chef judgment ("season to taste", "cook until done") as a formal concept.

## Problem-solving methods: TRIZ, Polya, Toyota standard work and A3, Cynefin

### Takeaway
These methods are meta-procedures. They tell the practitioner how to select or make a procedure. TRIZ maps a contradiction to a short list of principles. Polya asks "Do you know a related problem?". Toyota keeps a standard as the baseline for improvement. Cynefin is the most explicit selector: it tells the practitioner when best practice applies, when good practice applies, and when the practitioner must probe for emergent practice.

### Cited Findings
- **TRIZ matrix.** The contradiction matrix is 39 x 39. Each axis has the 39 engineering parameters. Each cell shows which of the 40 inventive principles can help with that contradiction. — [Triz.co.uk, Contradiction matrix glossary](https://www.triz.co.uk/glossary/contradiction-matrix)
- To use the matrix, select two parameters: the one to improve and the one to keep. The cell gives the most suitable principles. — [TU Braunschweig methodos, contradiction matrix](https://methodos.ik.ing.tu-bs.de/methode/WiderspruchsmatrixundInnovationsprinzipienausTRIZ.html?lang=en)
- From 1946 to 1985, Altshuller and his team analysed more than 400,000 patents to make the matrix. — [Triz40, What is TRIZ](https://triz40.com/triz-method.php)
- The 40 principles do not give ready solutions. They open directions of exploration, and the technical expertise of the user then changes them into concrete answers. They work well for simple to moderately difficult problems. — [TU Braunschweig methodos](https://methodos.ik.ing.tu-bs.de/methode/WiderspruchsmatrixundInnovationsprinzipienausTRIZ.html?lang=en); [Wikipedia, TRIZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRIZ)
- The Ideal Final Result (IFR) is the goal: the system gets the necessary function, and no system does it. In other words, the result occurs by itself. ARIZ (the Algorithm of Inventive Problem Solving) is a separate step-by-step procedure. — [Wikipedia, TRIZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRIZ); [Teaching TRIZ, contradiction matrix module](https://teaching-triz.netlify.app/content/triz/modules/90-triz-contradiction-matrix)
- **Polya.** *How to Solve It* has four phases: understanding the problem, devising a plan, carrying out the plan, and looking back. — [Wikipedia, How to Solve It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It); [Polya, *How to Solve It*, PDF](https://omi.fmi.uni-sofia.bg/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Polya_How-to-solve-it.pdf)
- The plan phase asks: "Do you know a related problem?" Other questions in that phase: have you seen the same problem in a slightly different form? Can you use the result or the method of a related problem? "Look at the unknown" tells the solver to find a familiar problem with the same or a similar unknown. — [Wikipedia, How to Solve It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It); [U. Kentucky, Polya list](https://www.ms.uky.edu/~lee/ma310sp15/polya)
- The no-fit rule in Polya: if you cannot solve the problem, first try to solve some related problem. An "auxiliary problem" is a side problem whose solution helps with the main problem. A variation of the problem can give a suitable auxiliary problem. — [Wikipedia, How to Solve It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It); [Polya PDF](https://omi.fmi.uni-sofia.bg/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Polya_How-to-solve-it.pdf)
- **Toyota standard work.** The form shows "the three elements that constitute standardized work": the current takt time (and cycle time), the work sequence, and the standard in-process stock. — [Lean Enterprise Institute, Standardized Work](https://www.lean.org/?p=1808)
- Without a current standard, a team has no stable baseline to judge if a change made the process better. An imperfect standard also gives a baseline to test and change. — [Rework, Standard work](https://resources.rework.com/fr/libraries/process-management/standard-work)
- In the 1992 Toyota view, kaizen is not an event. It is how the team stops the standard from becoming obsolete. — [Lean Blog, Toyota 1992](https://www.leanblog.org/2026/07/standardized-work-and-kaizen-toyota/)
- **A3.** The A3 puts the problem, the analysis, the corrective actions and the action plan on one sheet of A3 paper. It started at Toyota and became the standard format for problem-solving, proposals, plans and status reviews. — [Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/common/display/?o=2189)
- **Cynefin.** There are five domains: clear, complicated, complex, chaotic, and confusion at the centre. Clear and complicated are "ordered": cause and effect are known, or analysis can find them. Complex and chaotic are "unordered": cause and effect are known only in hindsight, or not at all. — [Wikipedia, Cynefin framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework) (citing Snowden & Boone, HBR 2007; Kurtz & Snowden, IBM Systems Journal 2003)

| Domain | Cause and effect | Sequence | Practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clear | Known, stable: "if you do X, expect Y" | Sense, categorize, respond | Best practice |
| Complicated | Needs analysis or expertise; several right answers | Sense, analyze, respond | Good practice |
| Complex | Known only in retrospect; no right answers | Probe, sense, respond ("safe to fail" experiments) | Emergent practice |
| Chaotic | No relations that you can see | Act, sense, respond | Novel practice |
| Confusion | Not known which domain applies | Divide into parts; assign each part to a domain | Depends on the parts |

  — [Wikipedia, Cynefin framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework)
- The names changed over time. Kurtz and Snowden (2003) used known, knowable, complex and chaotic. Snowden and Boone (2007) changed known and knowable to simple and complicated. Later, "simple" became "clear". — [Wikipedia, Cynefin framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework)
- The risk of the clear domain is complacency: "best practice is, by definition, past practice". Complacency can cause a "catastrophic" clockwise shift into chaos. Snowden and Boone recommend a channel, possibly anonymous, for people who disagree to warn about complacency. — [Wikipedia, Cynefin framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework)
- Critics say that the terms "known", "knowable", "sense" and "categorize" are ambiguous. People can disagree on where the boundaries between the domains are. — [Wikipedia, Cynefin framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynefin_framework)

### Inferences
- **Template:** TRIZ: a contradiction (improve A, keep B), then a matrix lookup, then principles, then the IFR as the target. Polya: four phases with a list of questions per phase. Toyota standard work: takt time, sequence, stock. A3: problem, analysis, countermeasures, plan, on one sheet.
- **Relations:** TRIZ is an index (the matrix) into a catalogue (40 principles), with ARIZ as the full algorithm for hard problems. Polya relates a new problem to solved problems by analogy and by auxiliary problems. Toyota relates the standard and the improvement in a cycle: the standard is the baseline, the A3 changes it, and the new standard replaces it. Cynefin is above all of them. It selects the kind of procedure.
- **Selection:** TRIZ selects by contradiction type. Polya selects by "same unknown". Cynefin selects by cause-and-effect type.
- **Split:** in TRIZ, the lookup is deterministic, and the change from principle to solution is judgment. Cynefin says that a deterministic procedure (best practice) is correct only in the clear domain.
- **No-fit rule:** each method has one. TRIZ: go from the matrix to ARIZ and the IFR. Polya: solve a related or auxiliary problem first. Toyota: if the standard does not work, find the problem with an A3, then write a new standard. Cynefin: if cause and effect are not known, probe with safe-to-fail experiments. If there is chaos, act first to make the situation stable. If the domain is not known, divide the problem into parts.

## Cross-discipline summary (inferences from the findings above)

| Discipline | Unit of procedure | Index key for selection | Deterministic part | Judgment part | Explicit no-fit rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aviation | Checklist (memory items + reference items) | Alert message; QRH index by condition | Item sequence | Order and combination of checklists | Aviate, navigate, communicate; captain combines checklists and uses judgment (Boeing, secondary source) |
| Military | Battle drill, SOP, mission order | Cue (drill); receipt of mission (MDMP) | Drill sequence | COA creation and choice | Disciplined initiative within commander's intent |
| Medicine | Algorithm box, checklist item, graded recommendation | Patient state; phase of the operation | Algorithm steps, timings | "Consider" items, weak recommendations | Weak recommendation: choose with patient values; WHO checklist "not intended to be comprehensive" |
| Cooking | Recipe, mother sauce, ratio | Base components of the dish | Ratio, technique | Seasoning, adjustment | Use the nearest base pattern and change one or two elements |
| Problem-solving | Principle, heuristic question, standard, domain | Contradiction; similar unknown; cause-and-effect type | Matrix lookup, standard work | Principle-to-solution change | ARIZ/IFR; auxiliary problem; A3 and new standard; Cynefin probe or act |

- Three common patterns are visible. (1) A short fixed core for the most dangerous items: memory items, killer items, battle drills. (2) An index from the situation to the procedure: an alert message, a cue, a rhythm, a contradiction. (3) A fallback that gives a purpose or a priority, not steps: aviate first, commander's intent, patient values, the IFR, the Cynefin domain.
- A second common pattern: the procedure base is extensible but has a protected core. WHO permits additions, but deletions decrease the effect. Small sauces add to a mother sauce. Toyota changes the standard only through an improvement cycle.
